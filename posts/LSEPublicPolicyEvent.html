Essay 21 May 2026 Lecture · Tariffs & Trade Policy

Reflection: Trade Under Strain — LSE Public Policy Review Event

There is something oddly enjoyable about hearing British and European academics talk about America. Perhaps it is simply the novelty of watching your own assumptions treated as objects of inquiry. For a North American, accustomed to American debates being the default frame through which everything else is interpreted, the reversal is refreshing. America becomes something to be explained rather than something taken for granted, and even familiar arguments acquire a different shape. The LSE panel on trade policy in a fractured world offered exactly this: a brisk, sharp, occasionally repetitive tour through the unravelling of the post-war economic order, with the United States firmly under the microscope.

The panel brought together three interdisciplinary perspectives on the current trade crisis, and taken together they offered a useful map of how the international order is fracturing. What emerged most clearly was the extent to which trade policy sits uneasily between two levels of politics. Internationally, trade arrangements are supposed to provide stability and constrain great powers through mutual expectations. Domestically, however, those same arrangements are sustained only insofar as they remain politically viable. The current crisis reflects the growing disconnect between these two logics. As domestic coalitions in the United States have shifted, the international order they once underwrote has become increasingly fragile.

The panel was organised around the launch of a special issue of the LSE Public Policy Review on trade policy, and it was the discussant, L. Alan Winters, who drew out what I found to be its most compelling thread by foregrounding Jeffry Frieden's contribution to the journal. Frieden's regime theory argument — that international orders unravel when anchor powers cease to behave as others expect — places what feels like contemporary chaos within a familiar historical pattern. Nixon's abandonment of Bretton Woods and the Bundesbank's role in the collapse of the European Monetary System both illustrate the point: international regimes break down when domestic political pressures in the dominant state make previous commitments unsustainable.

What makes this framework particularly satisfying is that it shifts the question from what is happening to why these regimes held together in the first place. The answer lies less in formal rules than in expectations, restraint, and the willingness of powerful states to accept constraints on their own freedom of action. In that sense, the current crisis appears less as a sudden rupture than as the consequence of changes within the United States itself — a point Winters underscored with an observation I had first heard Adam Tooze make earlier this year: Trump did not invigorate American right-wing radicalism in a vacuum; the people were already ready for that very culture to erupt.

Robert Falkner, approaching the question from an international relations perspective, offers a clean three-level framework for understanding the tariff escalation: the systemic rivalry between the US and China, domestic anti-globalisation sentiment, and individual leadership. The framework is useful. But his conclusion — that Trump's personal ideological commitment to tariffs explains the scale of what we are seeing — sits awkwardly next to the point Winters [and Tooze previously] raised. If the conditions were already there, Trump didn't make history. He just showed up for it.

This is where Boram Lee's work becomes an interesting contribution to the debate. Lee studies issue linkage: the strategy of attaching non-trade issues like labour or environmental standards to trade agreements to build legislative support for them. Her argument is that it only works when swing voters exist, because legislators only change their position on trade when their constituents can credibly reward or punish them for it. In a polarised electorate where people vote along party lines regardless of policy, that mechanism breaks down. The NAFTA case makes the point well — even in 1993, the environmental side deal wasn't enough on its own. The Clinton administration had to spend aggressively on pork barrel projects just to get the votes. If that's what it took then, the picture now is bleaker. What Lee is really showing is that the conditions for trade liberalisation didn't collapse because of Trump. They had already collapsed and he inherited the conditions.
